## Headline question — best candidate, or best-presenting?

**Best-presenting. On the evidence in the code, Tomáš hires the best-documented candidate,
and the surface will not tell him that's what he did.**

The argument, in the order the code makes it:

**1. The app knows what thin evidence is, and files it somewhere the decision never looks.**
`matching.py:723-767` is a well-built model of input poverty: fewer than three skills, unknown
education, no languages listed, an early-career paper trail without observed evidence — each
widens the band and records a recruiter-readable reason. Then every artefact that bears on the
pick ignores it. The sort is `compareScoreDesc(a.score, b.score)` (`group-eval-run.ts:453`).
The lead is `candidates[0]` (`:461`). The crown is column 0 (`ComparisonTable.tsx:192`). The
sealed decision record carries `score` and `robustness` but never `confidence`
(`:519-535`). Confidence appears in exactly one place: a row of the table
(`ComparisonTable.tsx:203`), between "overall fit" and "profile", as text. It is a **caption on
a decision that was already made without it.**

**2. Unequal input richness is not normalized — it is converted directly into score.**
`score_skills` accumulates `best * weight` per requirement (`matching.py:427`). No claim →
`best = 0.0` → a real subtraction from the total. There is no denominator for how much the
candidate wrote, no evidence-volume term anywhere in the scorer. The candidate with more
written material has more chances to clear `_MATCH_THRESHOLD` on each of N requirements, and
each miss is arithmetically identical to a demonstrated gap.

**3. Sparse is shown as weak, not as unknown — the exact conflation the question names.**
Three places, three confirmations. A must-have with no signal renders as a **red XCircle**
titled "missing must-have" (`ComparisonCells.tsx:120-126`) — the same mark as a candidate
assessed and found lacking. `CoverageCell` turns it into "0/4" in red (`:53-68`). And that
coverage row is a `leaderValue` row (`ComparisonTable.tsx:206-211`), so the best-documented
column gets the moss leader wash on the very row a manager scans to feel he's being fair. The
one mechanism that could have said "unknown" — the claimed-but-unproven bucket, which the app
built, tagged with reasons, and renders correctly in `AnalysisSummaryModal.tsx:197-211` — is
silently dropped on the way into the group eval (`group-eval-run.ts:425-431` copies seven
fields and not those three). So the third state collapses into the grey `Minus`, indistinguishable
from a row that never applied.

**4. The bias then compounds into the reason Tomáš is given for picking.**
`computeDifferentiators` (`group-eval-differentiators.ts:45-52`) crowns as a "unique strength"
any requirement skill the lead matched and no rival did. A rival who wrote little matched
little, so their silence becomes the lead's exclusivity. Those chips are then the headline
justification in the summary (`group-eval-run.ts:509-511`) **and the rationale sealed into the
permanent decision record** (`:526`). The best-documented candidate's advantage is thereby
laundered into a specific, confident, auditable sentence about why he was the right hire.

**5. Does the fairness tooling correct or label? It computes a correction and throws it away.**
This is the sharpest finding, because the correct thing is *already built*. `fairness_matrix`
(`matching.py:861-894`) re-scores every candidate under every candidate's weighting and ranks
by the mean — explicitly so that a candidate "who stays strong under everyone's weights is
robustly strong, not just flattered by their own". That mean-sorted `ranking` is computed, shipped
to the client, and rendered. And then `headlineOrder` is `recommendedOrder`, which is score order
(`GroupEvalModal.tsx:133` → `group-eval-run.ts:613`). When the two disagree, the panel prints one
amber sentence (`FairnessPanel.tsx:142-144`, `cs.json:2110`): *"the robust order differs from the
match order above — consider the matrix before deciding."* The crown does not move. The ranks do
not move. The AI headline does not move. The sealed record does not move. **The product computes
the fairer answer, displays the fairer answer, and ships the less fair one as the recommendation** —
delegating the reconciliation to a manager who by his own definition has fifteen minutes and won't
read a 6×6 grid. That is a fairness badge, not a fairness control.

And note what the check measures even when it works: `cs.json:2097` says so honestly — weighting
stability, not demographic bias. Nothing in this surface tests the axis this question is about.
The disparity that actually decides the hire — one candidate had more to show — is neither
measured nor labelled nor corrected. It is simply scored.

**The honest counter-argument, and why it loses.** The table is genuinely good, and a careful
reader is not defenceless: the confidence column is right there, each matched skill shows its
provenance and strength (`ComparisonCells.tsx:107-118`), unscored is a dash and never a zero,
and the coverage note says "top N of M". A patient evaluator could assemble the caveat themselves.
But this journey is not certified for a patient evaluator — it is certified for Tomáš, whose
character file defines him as the man who reads the recommendation and leaves
(`tomas-hiring-manager.md:16-29,61-69`). Every artefact he actually consumes — the crown, the
rank digit, the bolded AI headline, the "unique strengths" chips, the sealed rationale — is
computed on score alone. The evidence that would qualify them is placed beside them and given
no force. For this Character, at this speed, that is the same as not being there.

**Verdict: he picks the best-presenting candidate, believes the system verified it was fair,
and the record will say so in his name.**
